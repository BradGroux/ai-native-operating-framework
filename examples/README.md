# Framework Examples

## Purpose

This collection shows how the AI-Native Operating Framework can be applied to
different kinds of business work. Each example is a complete illustrative
standard operating procedure (SOP), accompanied by enough context to explain
how and why it was written.

The examples help people and machines understand the framework through clear
business documentation. They are not tests, proofs, required implementations,
tutorials, launch gates, or substitutes for an organization's own judgment.
They do not add requirements to the framework.

## How to Read an Example

The complete SOP is the primary artifact. Read it first as a business procedure,
then use the accompanying annotation to see how the procedure expresses the six
framework concerns.

Every example contains:

1. **Scenario overview** — the business setting, assumptions, purpose, and
   intended use of the example.
2. **Complete SOP** — a realistic procedure covering all eight areas required
   by the [SOP content standard](../framework/sop-content-standard.md), in a
   layout appropriate to the domain.
3. **Six-concern annotation** — a concise explanation of how the SOP addresses
   Intent, Responsibility, Work, Control, Assurance, and Learning.
4. **Provenance label** — a statement describing where the example content
   came from.
5. **Domain-specific boundary note** — a statement separating choices made for
   the example from requirements of the framework.

The annotation is explanatory. It points to relevant parts of the SOP so a
reader can see the framework in practice; it does not create a checklist of new
framework requirements. Likewise, roles, thresholds, time limits, controls, and
forms chosen for an example belong to that scenario unless the core framework
explicitly says otherwise.

The collection also demonstrates layout flexibility. Nine examples use
content-area headings to make comparison easy. The
[construction field-incident response](03-construction-field-incident-response.md)
and [employee onboarding and offboarding](04-employee-onboarding-offboarding.md)
examples instead follow their domains' natural incident-response and
joiner-mover-leaver structures. Both provide content traceability separately.
None of these structures is a required template.

## Provenance Labels

Every example uses one of these labels:

- **Fictional** — created for illustration and not presented as a description
  of any real organization or event.
- **Generalized** — derived from common operating patterns without representing
  a specific organization or event.
- **Sanitized from real work** — adapted from real operating material after
  confidential, personal, proprietary, unsafe, and identifying details have
  been removed or changed with appropriate permission.
- **Directly sourced** — based on identified source material that the
  contributor has the right to use and can attribute appropriately.

Provenance describes the origin of the content. It does not establish that the
example is accurate, approved, safe, lawful, or suitable for another
organization.

## Review Status

Every example also states one of these review statuses:

- **Illustrative; not domain-validated** — reviewed for clarity, completeness,
  internal consistency, publication safety, and framework alignment, but not
  reviewed by someone with the domain knowledge needed to support a claim of
  real-world or professional accuracy.
- **Domain-validated example** — reviewed by someone with relevant domain
  knowledge for the level of real-world or professional accuracy the example
  claims.

Domain validation is bounded by the stated reviewer, scope, and date. It is not
legal advice, professional certification, regulatory approval, or assurance
that the example fits another organization's circumstances.

Examples involving medicine, law, safety, regulation, finance, privacy, or
other high-impact work must not imply professional validation that did not
occur. Until relevant review is documented, they remain explicitly
illustrative and not domain-validated.

## Illustrative Operational Structures

The [shared operating memory file-structure companion](shared-operating-memory-file-structures.md)
shows five implementation patterns: minimal team, multi-team or portfolio,
controlled or regulated, time-bounded initiative, and federated memory.

It is not a twelfth SOP example and does not prescribe a repository hierarchy.
It demonstrates how the same operating-memory business requirements can be made
visible through unlike implementation structures.

## Publication Safety

An example may be accepted only when its contributor and maintainer have
reasonable grounds to believe it:

- contains no confidential, personal, proprietary, unsafe, or improperly
  licensed information;
- does not reveal credentials, security-sensitive details, or information that
  could identify a person unintentionally;
- has truthful provenance, attribution, permissions, and review status;
- distinguishes illustrative choices from professional or organizational
  requirements; and
- is written so readers are unlikely to mistake it for advice, authorization,
  or approval that it does not provide.

When a concern cannot be resolved through sanitization, attribution, permission,
or an explicit boundary, the example must not be published.
