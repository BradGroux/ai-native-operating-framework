## Working definition

The AI-Native Operating Framework is an open business operating framework and
method for defining, documenting, applying, and improving the standards and
procedures through which people and AI perform work.

## Resolved boundaries

- The framework concerns business operations, not technology architecture.
- It defines business standards and a practical method for applying them.
- It assumes people and AI may both perform business work under the same
  standards and SOPs.
- It does not require AI participation in every process or activity, and human
  accountability remains explicit.
- It defines standards that organizations apply to their existing business
  lifecycles and processes rather than prescribing one universal lifecycle.
- Technology may use the framework, but no tool, harness, model, protocol, or
  platform belongs to the framework.
- Applying the framework means organizing work according to its business
  standards. It does not mean adapting the framework to software.
- The same canonical documentation serves people and machines.
- Documentation should be clear, complete, consistent, and well structured
  because those qualities improve the business standard itself.
- No machine-specific schema, representation, protocol, adapter, profile, or
  integration is required.
- Interoperability concerns shared organizational understanding and consistent
  ways of working. It does not mean technical interoperability between tools.
- Portability follows from keeping business operations independent of tools. It
  is not a migration system that the framework must define.

## Locked strategic foundation

- The name is **AI-Native Operating Framework**.
- It launches as an open framework, not a proclaimed industry standard.
- Interoperability is a founding principle.
- It applies beyond software development to business operations generally.
- AI Dev Days teaches the framework.
- Digital Meld may apply the framework commercially but does not define its
  core.

## Current discovery focus

The framework organizes its standards around six business concerns:

1. **Intent** — purpose, scope, expected outcomes, and governing requirements.
2. **Responsibility** — ownership, roles, authority, and accountability.
3. **Work** — inputs, activities, outputs, dependencies, and handoffs.
4. **Control** — policies, decisions, approvals, risks, exceptions, escalation,
   and recovery.
5. **Assurance** — evidence, verification, quality, and completion.
6. **Learning** — review, feedback, change, and continual improvement.

These concerns define what business operations must make explicit without
prescribing a lifecycle or organizing the framework around document types.

## SOP content standard

Every SOP must make the following business content clear:

1. Purpose, scope, and expected outcome.
2. Accountable owner, participants, responsibilities, and decision authority.
3. Trigger, prerequisites, inputs, and authoritative sources.
4. Activities, decision points, dependencies, handoffs, and outputs.
5. Applicable policies, controls, approvals, and risks.
6. Exceptions, escalation, recovery, and stop conditions.
7. Completion criteria, verification, and required evidence.
8. Review ownership, review cadence or trigger, and change history.

Organizations may combine, rename, or arrange sections as appropriate. The
standard evaluates whether the business meaning is present and clear, not
whether a document follows one template.

## Standards maintenance method

Organizations create and maintain standards and SOPs through six practical
activities:

1. **Understand** — observe the actual work and gather governing requirements,
   evidence, and practitioner knowledge.
2. **Document** — describe the standard or SOP using the framework concerns and
   content requirements.
3. **Validate** — walk through normal work, decisions, handoffs, exceptions, and
   failure scenarios with the people responsible for the process.
4. **Approve** — obtain approval from the accountable business owner and any
   required policy or control authorities.
5. **Use** — make the approved standard available, communicate changes, and
   perform the work according to it.
6. **Improve** — review outcomes, evidence, exceptions, incidents, and changed
   requirements; revise and reapprove when warranted.

This method maintains operating standards. It does not prescribe the lifecycle
of the business processes those standards describe.

## Examples

The framework will include ten illustrative examples:

1. Accounts-payable invoice processing.
2. Software-change delivery.
3. Construction field-incident response.
4. Employee onboarding and offboarding.
5. M&A Day 1 transition.
6. Customer complaint and service recovery.
7. Regulatory-change implementation.
8. Supply-chain disruption response.
9. Sales proposal and contract approval.
10. Patient referral and care transition.

These examples show how the same framework concerns, SOP content standard, and
standards maintenance method apply across different business processes. They are
not tests, proofs, required implementations, or launch gates.

The framework will also provide an SOP for contributing additional examples.

Each example includes:

1. A short domain and scenario overview.
2. A complete, realistic SOP covering the eight required content areas in a
   layout appropriate to the domain.
3. A concise annotation showing how the SOP addresses Intent, Responsibility,
   Work, Control, Assurance, and Learning.
4. A label identifying whether the example is fictional, generalized, sanitized
   from real work, or directly sourced.
5. A note identifying domain-specific choices that are not universal framework
   requirements.

The SOP is the primary artifact. The additional context helps people and
machines understand how the framework applies without introducing a separate
machine-oriented design.

## Contributing examples

The framework will provide an SOP requiring contributors to:

1. Confirm that a proposed example adds a useful domain or operating pattern
   without unnecessary duplication.
2. Disclose whether it is fictional, generalized, sanitized from real work, or
   directly sourced.
3. Remove confidential, personal, proprietary, unsafe, or improperly licensed
   material.
4. Produce a complete illustrative SOP satisfying the eight content
   requirements.
5. Add the scenario overview, six-concern annotation, provenance label, and
   domain-specific boundary note.
6. Review the example for business clarity, internal consistency, and framework
   alignment.
7. Obtain relevant domain review when claiming professional or real-world
   accuracy; otherwise label the example illustrative and not domain-validated.
8. Provide contributor attribution, a responsible maintainer, and known review
   or update triggers.
9. Address maintainer feedback before acceptance.
